Recommended by the Machine

Your next customer isn't going to scroll through ten blue links. They're going to ask an AI who's best, and it's going to name two or three businesses. This is about how it decides — and why you can't tell where you stand by looking at your own website.

Somebody within ten miles of you needs exactly what you sell. Right now. Two years ago they'd have typed it into Google, seen a page of results, and picked from the first handful. Today they open ChatGPT — or the AI answer sitting on top of Google — and type it like a question to a friend: who's the best one near me?

And they don't get a page. They get an answer. Two names, maybe three, in a short paragraph that sounds like advice.

There's no page two. There's no scrolling to find you at number seven. You're either in the sentence or you don't exist.

"Ranking was a list you could be far down on. Being recommended is a door that's either open or shut."

The machine can only recommend what it can read

Here's the part that surprises people: an AI isn't judging whether you're good at your job. It has no way to know that. It's judging whether it can reach you, understand you, and verify you — and if it can't do all three, it moves on to a competitor it can.

Four things quietly decide it:

  • Can it get in? A huge number of sites actively block AI crawlers without anyone realising — a line in a file, a security setting, a plugin default. You can't be recommended from behind a locked door.
  • Can it understand what you are? Structured data is how a page says "this is a business, here's where it is, here's what it does, here's what it costs" in a form a machine reads without guessing.
  • Can it verify you exist? A claim on your own website is just a claim. Corroboration elsewhere — your business profile, directories, other people's pages — is what turns it into a fact worth repeating.
  • Can it lift a clean answer out of you? Models quote passages. A page that answers a real question in plain language gets quoted. A page of brochure adjectives has nothing to lift.

None of that is about being a better plumber, or a better lawyer, or a better anything. It's about being legible to a machine that never visits your shop.

You cannot see any of this by looking at your website

This is the trap, and almost everybody falls in it.

You open your site. It loads. It looks good — you paid for it to look good. The pictures are nice, the phone number's right, and you close the tab reassured. Meanwhile the four things above are all invisible from where you're standing. They live in files you've never opened, in headers no visitor sees, in how the page renders for a robot rather than a human, in whether Google has actually indexed the page or merely knows it's there.

A site can look immaculate and be completely invisible to the thing now doing the recommending. That's not a rare edge case. That's the normal condition of most small business websites in 2026.

"Your website looking fine is not evidence. It's the thing that stops people checking."

Which is exactly why the tools matter

You can't eyeball this, and you can't guess at it. Every one of these is a measurement — either the crawler is blocked or it isn't, either the structured data validates or it doesn't, either your page loads in 1.8 seconds on a phone on real-world data or it takes 6 and people leave before it appears.

Proper tools go and check all of it, on every page, and come back with a list. Not opinions — findings, each one either true or false about your specific site today. That's the whole difference between an audit and a sales pitch.

What actually gets measured
Access
Whether Google's crawler and the AI crawlers can reach your pages at all — and whether anything is quietly telling them not to.
Understanding
Whether your structured data exists, validates, and describes the right things — the difference between appearing as a rich result and appearing as a grey link.
Speed
Core Web Vitals from real-world field data, not a lab guess. What your site actually does on a mid-range phone on mobile data.
Citability
Whether your pages contain passages an AI can lift and attribute, or whether there's nothing quotable on the whole site.
Corroboration
Whether anyone outside your own domain confirms you exist — profiles, citations, links, and whether any of them are actively hurting you.
The gap
What the business currently being recommended instead of you has that you don't. Usually it's two or three specific, fixable things.

Run that once and the fog clears. You stop arguing about opinions and start working a list, in order, from the thing costing you the most money down to the thing that barely matters. Clarity is what makes speed possible — you can't move fast on a problem you can't see.

The part with a clock on it

Search used to reshuffle constantly. You could be nowhere in March and everywhere in June. Recommendations don't work like that.

When a model has been picking the same two businesses in your town for a year — because those two were legible and you weren't — that pattern hardens. Their name is in the answer, in the citations, in the pages that get written about your industry, in the training data of whatever comes next. You're not competing with their marketing budget. You're competing with a year of being the obvious answer.

The businesses winning this right now, in most towns, are not the best ones. They're the ones whose websites happened to be readable. That's a frustrating thing to hear and a very good thing to know — because it's fixable, and it was never really technical. It's a checklist somebody has to actually run.
